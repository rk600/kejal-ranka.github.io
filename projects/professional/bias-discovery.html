The $5M Bug That Didn't Throw an Error

A hidden assumption was silently producing wrong answers for months. Everyone trusted the metric. The metric was lying.

← Back to portfolio

🎯 Situation

A new operational program had launched. Significant investment — interventions, outreach, follow-ups. The performance metric told a discouraging story: ~30% effectiveness. Barely worth the cost.

Leadership paused expansion. Why scale something that doesn't work?

But the operators on the ground disagreed. They could see improvements in individual cases. The numbers just didn't match what they observed every day.

The red flag: When experienced operators say "this is working" and the metric says "this isn't working" — one of them is wrong. My bet: the metric.

⚡ Task

Figure out why the metric contradicts reality. Either:

  • The program genuinely isn't working (operators are wrong) → we should cut investment
  • The measurement is flawed (metric is wrong) → we're leaving $5M+ on the table

Stakes: leadership decisions worth millions were hanging on this answer.

🔧 Action

I started with one question: "How do we define 'post-intervention'?"

The answer: any transaction delivered after the intervention date counts as post-intervention performance.

For most programs, this works. Intervention → next delivery in 3-5 days → clean measurement.

But this program was different. 2-3 month lead times between creation and delivery.

THE BUG: Timeline for a single transaction: Jan 15: Transaction CREATED (before any intervention) Feb 1: INTERVENTION happens Mar 20: Transaction DELIVERED (after intervention) The metric counted this as "post-intervention" ← WRONG. The entity hadn't changed behavior. It just took 2 months to deliver. // Hundreds of pre-intervention transactions were contaminating // the "post" window → metric shows 30% instead of 80%+ ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ THE FIX: Use CREATION DATE, not DELIVERY DATE. Only count transactions created AFTER the intervention. Now measuring actual behavior change, not pipeline timing. Result: Program effectiveness = 80%+ (not 30%)

Why Nobody Caught It

  • The formula produced numbers. No errors, no nulls, ran on schedule. The most dangerous bugs don't crash.
  • Other programs were fine. With 3-5 day lead times, the bias was negligible. This was the first long-lead program.
  • The assumption was invisible. Nowhere documented. Just "how it's always been done."
  • Confirmation bias. "The program isn't working" was a plausible story. Nobody questioned the measurement.

The Investigation Timeline

Day 1

Operators say it's working. Metric says it isn't. I decide to trust the operators.

Day 2

Map the full measurement methodology. Ask: "what implicit assumptions does this make?"

Day 3

Realize delivery date ≠ creation date for this program. Hypothesis: lead-time contamination.

Day 4-5

Build parallel calculation. Compare 6 months of data. Hypothesis confirmed: 80% vs 30%.

Day 6

Present to stakeholders. Campaign expansion resumes immediately.

Week 3

Develop "Improvement Index" as a general-purpose replacement. Roll out to 5 teams.

The "Improvement Index": Current Year Rate − Previous Year Rate. Immune to lead-time bias AND seasonal distortion. Became the standardized way teams measure intervention effectiveness, replacing 3 bespoke calculations. Influenced 2026 org-wide goal-setting.

🧠 The Meta-Lesson

This wasn't a sophisticated statistical problem. It was a wrong assumption that nobody questioned because the system "worked."

Three principles I took away:

  • Trust domain experts over dashboards. When metrics contradict experienced operators, investigate the measurement first.
  • The most dangerous bugs produce plausible wrong answers. A crash is easy to fix. A number that looks reasonable but is wrong can persist for months.
  • Don't iterate on the operation — question the measurement. The team was trying harder interventions. The fix was in the formula, not the program.
