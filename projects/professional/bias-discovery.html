The $5M Bug Nobody Knew Existed

A lead-time assumption buried in a calculation was silently making a program look like it wasn't working. For months.

The Situation

A new operational program had launched. The team was investing significant resources — interventions, outreach, follow-ups. But the performance metrics told a discouraging story: the program appeared to have ~30% effectiveness. Barely worth the investment.

Leadership paused expansion. Why scale something that's not working?

But the operators on the ground disagreed. They could SEE the improvements in individual cases. The numbers just... didn't reflect reality.

The red flag I noticed: When experienced operators say "this is working" and the metric says "this isn't working" — the metric is probably wrong. Operators have domain intuition that metrics don't.

The Investigation

I started by asking a simple question: how do we define "post-intervention"?

The answer: any transaction that is delivered after the intervention date counts as "post-intervention performance."

For most programs, this is fine. Intervention happens → next delivery is 3-5 days later → measurement is clean.

But this program was different. Its transactions have 2-3 month lead times between creation and delivery.

THE BUG: Timeline: Jan 15: Transaction CREATED (before intervention) Feb 1: INTERVENTION happens Mar 20: Transaction DELIVERED (after intervention) The metric counted this as "post-intervention" ← WRONG The transaction was created BEFORE the intervention. The entity hadn't changed behavior yet — it just took 2 months to deliver. Impact: Hundreds of pre-intervention transactions were contaminating the "post" measurement window → making the program look ineffective.

Why Nobody Caught It

  • The formula "worked" — it produced numbers, didn't error, ran on schedule. The most dangerous bugs are the ones that don't throw errors.
  • Other programs were fine — with 3-5 day lead times, the bias was negligible. This was the first long-lead-time program.
  • The assumption was implicit — nowhere in the documentation did it say "this assumes short lead times." It was just... how it had always been done.
  • Confirmation bias — "the program isn't working" was a plausible explanation, so nobody questioned the measurement.

The Fix

Immediate: Switch from delivery date to creation date

Only count transactions created after the intervention — regardless of when they're delivered. This ensures we're measuring actual behavior change, not pipeline timing.

Validation: 6-month parallel comparison

I ran both methodologies (old vs new) on 6 months of historical data. The corrected methodology showed the program was performing at 80%+ effectiveness — not the ~30% the biased metric suggested.

Prevention: The "Improvement Index"

I developed a new metric — Current Year Rate minus Previous Year Rate — that's inherently immune to this class of bias. By comparing like-for-like periods (Jan vs Jan, Q1 vs Q1), it eliminates both lead-time contamination AND seasonal distortion in a single formula.

The Improvement Index was adopted as a standardized metric across the organization. It became the primary way teams measure intervention effectiveness, replacing 3 different bespoke calculations.

The Investigation Timeline

Day 1

Operators say "this is working but metrics don't show it." I decide to trust the operators.

Day 2

Map out the full measurement methodology. Ask: "what assumptions does this make?"

Day 3

Realize delivery date ≠ creation date for this program. Hypothesis: lead-time contamination.

Day 4-5

Build parallel calculation (creation date vs delivery date). Compare 6 months of data. Hypothesis confirmed: 80% vs 30%.

Day 6

Present findings to stakeholders. Campaign expansion resumed.

Week 3

Develop Improvement Index as a general solution. Roll out to 5 teams.

Impact

  • $5M+ unblocked — campaign expansion resumed after proving the program was effective all along
  • Methodology standardized — the fix and Improvement Index adopted across 5 teams
  • Systematic audit triggered — all time-based metrics reviewed for similar lead-time assumptions
  • Trust restored — operators and metrics aligned again; leadership confidence in data recovered

The Meta-Lesson

This wasn't a sophisticated statistical problem. It was a wrong assumption that nobody questioned because the system "worked." The hardest bugs aren't the ones that crash — they're the ones that produce plausible-looking wrong answers.

My takeaway: whenever a metric contradicts ground-truth human observation, investigate the measurement first. The humans are usually right.
